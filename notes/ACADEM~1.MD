Citations give brief details of the source at the point in the text where the source is used.

Citations using a numeric system, such as **IEEE**, show numbers in the text which refer to a numerical sequence of references at the end of the document. The numbers are shown using brackets; for example:

> Oakshott [1] argues that
> 

The details of author, title, date etc, and page number (if appropriate, e.g. if a quotation has been used) are included in the numbered reference; for example:

[1]. K. Oakshott, *Book title*. New York: Publishing House, 2001, p3.

> Gill reports that recent research "revealed what researchers had long suspected, that man-made sound had a negative effect on these deep-diving whales" [1].
> 

[1] V. Gill (2013, July 3). *Blue and Beaked Whales Affected by Simulated Navy Sonar*. BBC News [Online]. Available: http://www.bbc.co.uk/news/science-environment-23115939. [Accessed: July 3, 2013].

## **What do the references include?**

The common features for each of these examples is the inclusion of:

- The authors' family name and at least first initial
- The article title
- The journal title
- The year the article was published
- The volume number of the journal (and in most cases the issue number within the volume)
- The first page of the article (if not the last page as well)

In IEEE referencing style the references are listed in the order they were first cited, where as in other referencing styles they are listed in alphabetical order.

Secondary references: If you wish to cite a reference that a source you are reading has referred to, but you have not read yourself, you need to cite both sources in-text. For example, you are reading a textbook by Smith (2007) and the book cites Craig (1995). You could refer to this in the following way:

- **IEEE**: Craig, as cited in Smith [3] argued that...

The bibliography would usually include only the reference to Smith, as this was the text you read.

It is also important to think carefully before using a secondary reference, as you are dealing with second-hand knowledge. You need to be able to trust that the source says what it is being reported as saying and that it is being used appropriately for the argument you are making. Ideally you should consult the original wherever possible.

# Frequently Asked Questions

| **Q1** | **What's the difference between quoting and paraphrasing?** |
| --- | --- |
| A1 | When you quote, you copy text word-for-word, surround it by quotation marks, and include a citation and reference to acknowledge the original author.
When you paraphrase, you put someone else's ideas into your own words. There is no need for quotation marks but you still need a citation and reference.
Paraphrasing demonstrates that you have really understood the original text and are able to incorporate the ideas into your own work. It's not just about changing one or two words. |
|  |  |
| **Q2** | **What is meant by 'other people's work'?** |
| A2 | Appropriate referencing is about giving credit to other people for their own work. 'Other people's work' may include their ideas, written work, artwork, dance choreography, research data, and so on. This may be published (e.g. in books or websites) or unpublished (e.g. other students' essays). |
|  |  |
| **Q3** | **Can I use photographs found on a website?** |
| A3 | Photographs are an example of 'other people's work' and need to be properly acknowledged using a citation and reference. If the photographs are also protected by copyright (which is often the case with images and music), you may also need to ask the permission of the copyright owner before using them. [Guidance from the library on copyright issues is given here](https://www.york.ac.uk/records-management/copyright/). |
|  |  |
| **Q4** | **Isn't it better to quote authoritative sources word-for-word?** |
| A4 | It is often helpful to quote authoritative sources, provided you give appropriate acknowledgement. But remember that quoting other people is not enough to get good marks. You will be awarded marks for showing that you have done background reading and can cite authoritative sources, and for demonstrating that you understand the subject material by giving your own opinions. |
|  |  |
| **Q5** | **What's the difference between collaboration and collusion?** |
| A5 | Collaboration means working with others in ways authorised or required within the terms of the assignment. For example, collaboration may be informal (e.g. during student discussions) or more formal (e.g. as a way of completing group assignments). For group assignments, it is usually necessary to ensure that individual contributions can be clearly identified when submitting the work. This depends on what is required by the lecturer setting the work. Collusion, on the other hand, means working with others in ways not authorised within the terms of the assignment. For example, if you jointly write an essay with a fellow student and each submit a very similar essay individually as though it were your own work, this would be called collusion.
Legitimate collaboration is an excellent teaching and learning tool if done properly. As a student, you have the right to clear guidelines from your lecturer. However, once these have been provided, it is up to you to comply with them and ensure that the specifics and spirit of the guidelines are followed.
York students should note that there may be slightly different guidance for modules in different departments. It is up to you to ensure that you understand the rules in force for a particular module. As always, if in doubt, ask! |
|  |  |
| **Q6** | **Can I just list all my sources in the reference list?** |
| A6 | A reference list at the end of your assignment by itself is not enough. You also need to cite brief details of the source in-text - i.e., at the point in your work where you refer to the original source. |
|  |  |
| **Q7** | **How much is 'too much' quoting?** |
| A7 | There is no definitive rule for calculating 'too much' quoting, and you should seek the advice of your course lecturer. As a student studying at university you are certainly expected to incorporate your own thoughts and insights into your work. |
|  |  |
| **Q8** | **How do I acknowledge online sources?** |
| A8 | In most referencing systems, online sources must be acknowledged by including the web address (i.e., the URL), and the date the website was accessed. It's sometimes difficult to find the date that a webpage was produced. In those cases you can usually use the term 'n.d.' which means 'no date'. The precise format will depend on the reference system stipulated by your department. |
|  |  |
| **Q9** | **How do I acknowledge personal communications?** |
| A9 | You should acknowledge people who have given you interesting ideas. For example, if you are writing an essay on Elvis Presley, and you exchange emails with an expert on the topic who's writing Elvis' biography, an appropriate citation and reference might look something like this.Elvis Presley was always a bit of an outsider, and as a teenager he cultivated that image by wearing long hair, straggly sideburns and ostentatious clothes (Smith, 2006).Reference:Smith, J., 2006. Personal email communication.
You should seek permission from the author before using him or her as a reference. |
|  |  |
| **Q10** | **Do I really have to read all the stuff I reference?** |
| A10 | It really depends on how you reference your reading. Reference lists are used to give details of work which you have read for yourself. However, in some circumstances you can use a secondary reference. |
|  |  |
| **Q11** | **How do I acknowledge a secondary reference?** |
| A11 | Let's suppose you're reading about the French Revolution in a book by Claude Noir (written in 2002) and he refers to the number of executions, citing as his source the work of Marie Vert (written in 1999).
If you use these statistics but do not read the original book by Marie Vert, you must still indicate how you obtained the statistics; for example:
One hundred people were executed by Monsieur Mince before the populace decided he should be executed for treason (Vert, 1999, cited in Noir, 2002)
Reference: Noir, C., Tales from the Guillotine. Paris: Cutting Press. 2002 |
|  |  |
| **Q12** | **What do I do if I cannot find the source for a quotation or point which I want to include in my essay?** |
| A12 | (a) If it is a quotation, paraphrase it and then treat it as (b). This is because you should never use direct quotations without checking that they are correct.
(b) Include the point but make clear in the body of your essay or with a footnote that it is not your own. E.g. “As has been pointed out before, freewill is worthless without real choices." |

Top tips

- Carefully read assignment tasks, information in your department handbook and any other guidance about what is acceptable and unacceptable assessment practice.
- Manage your time effectively: try to plan ahead and start assignments early so you don’t succumb to last-minute panic. If you are having problems, talk to your department.
- Question what you read: is this a reliable source?
- Keep your notes: it is always useful to have these, especially if you need to prove how you wrote your essay later on and what individual contribution you might have made to a group task.
- Essay writing services: just don’t! Research has shown that the quality of the essays can be terrible. The University takes this matter very seriously so it is just not worth the risk.
- Reference web resources as scrupulously as you would books and journals.
- Carefully paraphrase and quote material: a footnote or in-text citation is not enough on its own to indicate that the words are not your own. Either put the words in quotation marks or paraphrase sufficiently.
- Don’t just copy and paste: get into the habit of good practice by not copying and pasting directly into documents from the internet.
- Use the resources available to help manage your research and to ensure the integrity of your assignments. For example: EndNote Web, Turnitin or the [Integrity website](http://www.york.ac.uk/integrity/).
